Household books: where to look for Soviet-era ancestors in Belarus

If you are looking for relatives who lived in a Belarusian village in the Soviet period, do not stop at parish registers and civil registration. There is another source that often shows a whole household, not a single entry.

Vitaly Khoruzhko · 16 September 2026

If you are looking for relatives who lived in a Belarusian village in Soviet times, do not limit yourself to parish books and civil registration (ZAGS).

There is another source that can say a great deal about a family — household books (pokhozyaystvennye knigi).

For a researcher they are especially useful because they show not one person but a whole household: who lived in the yard, who belonged to the family, when its composition changed, and where relatives may have gone.

What household books are

Household books were kept by village soviets (selsoviets) to record people and farms.

Depending on the period and the specific volume, you may find:

  • surname, given name and patronymic;
  • year or date of birth;
  • family composition;
  • notes on relationships;
  • place of residence;
  • education;
  • occupation;
  • farm details;
  • other data on household members.

For genealogy, household composition is especially valuable.

Suppose you never find a birth entry for your great-grandfather. A household book may still show him, his wife, children and other relatives — and those people can lead to the next document.

In genealogy you do not always need a document about the ancestor themselves. Sometimes a relative’s record finds them for you.

When to look for household books

For Belarus, the Soviet period is especially relevant.

The first postwar household books in the BSSR began to be compiled from late 1944.

Earlier prewar volumes survived much less well, so do not count on a complete run from the 1930s.

If your family lived in a Belarusian village after the war, household books belong on the list of sources to check.

The interesting part: where are they kept?

This is where real archival investigation begins.

It is easy to assume:

“Household books are selsoviet records. So I should write to the selsoviet.”

Sometimes that works. Often it does not.

After the war a large share of household books was transferred to regional (oblast) archives. Later, some volumes returned to selsoviets.

Today, books for the same period or district may sit in different places.

For example:

selsoviet → district archive → zonal archive → oblast archive

Or only part of that chain.

So the answer:

“We do not have such books”

does not mean:

“Those books do not exist.”

It only means:

“They are not here.”

Why you cannot search only by today’s map

There is another trap.

Your ancestor may have lived in a village that today belongs to one district and selsoviet. In the 1950s or 1960s the administrative map could have been different.

What changed:

  • districts;
  • selsoviets;
  • oblasts;
  • place names;
  • territorial subordination.

Before searching, try to restore the administrative history of the village. Only then ask where the records of that selsoviet may have ended up.

That is one reason Soviet-period genealogy often becomes an investigation of its own.

Selsoviet or archive?

Check both tracks.

If the books survive in the selsoviet, a request there may be the simplest path. If not, move to archives — and not only the oblast archive.

Records may be held in:

  • an oblast archive;
  • a zonal archive;
  • a district archive;
  • the archival fond of a former selsoviet;
  • another institution that received transfers.

So do not ask only:

“Do you have household books?”

Ask:

“Where are the household books of such-and-such selsoviet for such-and-such years?”

That is a different request.

Another issue: access to personal data

Household books contain personal information, so kinship proof may be required — especially for relatively recent entries.

Before writing, prepare a kinship chain:

me → parent → grandparent → great-grandparent → the family you need

And hold documents that support that chain.

If the book you need is missing

Do not stop.

Look for other records of the same period, for example:

  • civil registration;
  • population lists;
  • censuses;
  • military papers;
  • school records;
  • kolkhoz and sovkhoz files;
  • land records;
  • passport questionnaires;
  • relatives’ papers.

Cross-checking several sources is especially useful, for example:

  • a household book;
  • a marriage entry;
  • a child’s birth entry;
  • a military record;
  • a passport questionnaire.

Gradually you get not just a name but a documented family link.

Search for a family, not only a person

This is the main practical tip.

If you know:

Ivan Petrovich, born about 1910, village N

do not search only for:

Ivan Petrovich

Try to find the Petrov household.

If it lists:

  • Ivan;
  • Maria — wife;
  • Nikolai — son;
  • Anna — daughter,

you immediately have new search leads.

And if the next volume shows a changed household, that is also information: someone arrived, someone left, someone became head of household, someone moved away.

Family history is rebuilt from change over time, not only from isolated entries.

A household book is a snapshot of a family in time

That is why the source matters.

A parish entry may say:

“Ivan was born.”

Years later a household book may show:

where Ivan lived, with whom, who his children were, and what the farm looked like.

Several volumes across years already show a family in motion. Combined with maps, parish books, civil registration, archival files and DNA matches, the picture becomes much fuller.

If you are searching for ancestors in Belarus

Start with four things:

  1. place;
  2. approximate period;
  3. surname and spelling variants;
  4. known relatives.

Then establish:

which selsoviet the village belonged to in that period → where that selsoviet’s records are held → which books survive → which other sources can confirm what you find.

That is how the absence of one document becomes not a dead end but the next search point.

Heritavia

In genealogical research we do not stop at one database or one record type. We cross-check archival sources, relatives’ papers, old maps, administrative history and other evidence to rebuild a family story.

Sometimes one household book is enough to restart the search for a family line.
